Exploring the Intersection of Organic Materials and Technology

12 jan 20252025 年 1 月 12 日 · 3 min read约 3 分钟读完

这篇文章写于在学校的课程期间，原文是英文。

Overview

In the ever-evolving field of creative computing, I am interested in applying organic materials to technology, not only for aesthetic or design purposes, but also to address some of the world’s most pressing environmental issues.

(Source: Hair Cuttery, n.d., Hair Cuttery Blog, Available at: https://blog.haircuttery.com/all-posts/a-guide-to-different-types-of-hair-treatments/).

Why do I choose hair？

Among these materials, hair is one that I find particularly interesting. Although it may sound a bit weird, existing research has shown that hair has a surprising adsorptive capacity, especially for oil pollutants in the ocean. This makes it a promising organic material in environmental cleanup technologies.

Hair, as a byproduct of human biology, surprisingly exhibits high efficiency in adsorbing and capturing pollutants such as oil. Research has shown that hair can be used for oil spill cleanup because of its strong affinity for hydrocarbons. When placed in polluted water, hair can adsorb oil and help remove pollutants from the water body. This adsorptive capacity makes hair a valuable resource for addressing issues such as marine pollution and oil spills (Beckett, 2015).

From a technical perspective, using hair as a material for environmental solutions is highly appealing. Hair is biodegradable, renewable, and lightweight, making it an ideal choice for various environmental applications. By braiding hair or incorporating it into synthetic fibres to create materials that can adsorb pollutants, we can develop technologies that maximise natural properties while minimising the negative impact on the environment (Jang et al., 2014).


One of the most attractive uses of hair in technology is to create adsorbent materials for cleaning up oil spills. Researchers have experimented with using human and animal hair to make mats or oil-absorbing buoys that can be deployed in waters affected by oil spills. These hair-based materials are not only cost-effective but also environmentally sustainable, as they can be recycled and used multiple times. By integrating hair into more advanced technological systems, we can enhance its effectiveness and versatility in cleaning up various pollutants (Jang et al., 2014; Beckett, 2015).


I believe hair also has potential in the fields of wearable and biodegradable technologies. For example, integrating hair fibers into clothing or accessories that respond to environmental changes, such as detecting pollutants or water quality changes, can prompt us to develop environmentally responsive wearable devices. These devices can act as sensors to detect pollution levels and even change their properties according to environmental conditions (Jang et al., 2014).

The integration of organic materials like hair with creative computing opens new possibilities. With its natural pollutant adsorption properties, hair can be used not only for environmental solutions but also for interactive installations that respond to pollution or the environment. Imagine an interactive installation where the hair-based material changes colour or texture upon contact with polluted water or air, potentially providing an intuitive display of environmental health. Such interactive projects can not only raise people’s awareness of environmental issues but also showcase the potential of organic materials in technology.
