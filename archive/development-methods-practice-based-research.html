Week 1 Research: How Do I Approach Practice-Based Research?

3 oct 20262026 年 10 月 3 日

这篇文章写于在学校的课程期间，原文是英文。

This blog is where I explore practice-based research. According to Candy (2006), research is practice-based when a creative artefact is the basis of the contribution to knowledge. In other words, the making is part of the research, not just the result of it. That means the way a project is developed matters as much as what is made. So this week I looked at five common development methods, and asked which one fits a practice-based way of working, and which one I would use for my own final project, a technology project this year.

Five methods

01 · Linear (waterfall)

Linear (waterfall) goes through requirements, design, build, test and launch once, in order. It is easy to plan, but feedback arrives at the very end, when changes are most expensive. Interestingly, even Royce (1970), whose paper is often blamed for the waterfall, recommended building the whole thing twice, so that the version delivered is really the second one (Larman and Basili, 2003).

02 · V-model

The V-model keeps the linear order but pairs every design stage with a matching test stage (Forsberg and Mooz, 1991). It suits projects with fixed, safety-critical requirements, not open questions.

03 · Spiral

The spiral model repeats loops of planning, risk analysis, building and evaluation, each loop larger than the last (Boehm, 1988). I like that it puts risk first, but it is designed for large engineering teams.

04 · Agile (Scrum)

Agile, for example Scrum, works in short, fixed “sprints”, each ending with a working increment and a review (Beck et al., 2001; Schwaber and Sutherland, 2020). It is powerful, but its ceremonies assume a team, and I mostly work alone.

05 · Iterative

Iterative development is simpler: make a rough version, test it with people, reflect, and make a better version. Larman and Basili (2003) show it has been used since the 1950s, and that all its variations share one aim: avoiding a single-pass, document-driven process.

Why I choose iterative

For my final project, I will work iteratively. The main reason is that, as in practice-based research, I will not know the answer at the start; the making is how I find it. Schön (1983) calls this “reflection-in-action”: the practitioner learns by having a conversation with the situation. A linear plan would make me pretend to know the outcome in week one. Iteration turns each prototype into a small experiment, and each round of user feedback into evidence I can document in this blog.

I will still borrow from the others: a simple timeline and fixed deadlines from the linear model, regular risk checks from the spiral, and two-week cycles from agile. My plan is about five iterations, each ending with testing and a written reflection.
