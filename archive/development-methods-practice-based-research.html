Week 1 Research: Choosing a Development Method for My Final Project

3 oct 20262026 年 10 月 3 日

这篇文章写于在学校的课程期间，原文是英文。

This blog is where I explore practice-based research. According to Candy (2006), research is practice-based when a creative artefact is the basis of the contribution to knowledge. In other words, the making is part of the research, not just the result of it. That means the way a project is developed matters as much as what is made. So this week I looked at five common development methods, and asked which one fits a practice-based way of working, and which one I would use for my own final project, a technology project this year.

Five methods

01 · Linear (waterfall)

How it works. The waterfall model moves through a fixed sequence of stages: gather requirements, design the whole system, build it, test it, and finally launch and maintain it. Each stage is finished and signed off before the next one starts, usually with a document that the next stage depends on. The name comes from the way work flows downwards and, ideally, never goes back up.

Strengths. It is easy to understand, easy to schedule and easy to report on, because at any moment you know exactly which stage you are in. It works well when the requirements are clear and unlikely to change, for example rebuilding something that already exists or delivering to a fixed contract.

Weaknesses. Feedback arrives at the very end. Real users see the product only after it is built, when changes are the most expensive, and a wrong assumption made in week one can survive until the final week. Interestingly, even Royce (1970), whose paper is usually blamed for the waterfall, warned about this and recommended building the whole thing twice, so that the version finally delivered is really the second one (Larman and Basili, 2003).

For my project. A student technology project almost never has fixed requirements in week one. If I planned everything up front, I would probably discover in testing that the core idea needs to change, with no time left to change it.

02 · V-model

How it works. The V-model keeps the linear order but bends it into a V (Forsberg and Mooz, 1991). The left side goes down through levels of definition, from user needs to system design to detailed design. The bottom of the V is the build. The right side climbs back up through matching levels of testing: each piece is tested against its detailed design, the system against the system design, and finally the whole product is accepted against the original user needs. The dashed lines in the diagram show these pairs.

Strengths. Testing is planned from the start rather than added at the end, and every requirement has a clear way to be checked. This is why the V-model is common in safety-critical fields such as medical devices, cars and aerospace, where every requirement must be verified and documented.

Weaknesses. It is still a single pass. It verifies that the product matches the plan, but it cannot tell you whether the plan was the right idea in the first place. It is also heavy on documentation.

For my project. I like the idea of pairing every goal with a test, and I want to keep that habit. But the full V-model is built for answering “did we build it right?”, while my early questions are “are we building the right thing?”.

03 · Spiral

How it works. Boehm (1988) designed the spiral model as a response to the problems of the waterfall. The project moves around a spiral, and every loop passes through four activities: set the objectives for this round, identify and reduce the biggest risks (often by building a prototype), develop and test, and plan the next loop. Each loop goes further out, which means more of the product is built and more money and time are committed.

Strengths. It is driven by risk. Instead of asking “what is the next stage?”, it asks “what is the most likely thing to make this project fail, and how can we find out cheaply?”. Prototypes are used early, so problems surface before they become expensive.

Weaknesses. Each loop includes formal risk analysis and planning, which suits large, long and expensive engineering projects with teams who can afford that management work. For a small project, the overhead can be bigger than the project itself.

For my project. The risk-first thinking is very useful. At the start of each round I want to ask what is most uncertain, for example whether a sensor is accurate enough, and test that first.

04 · Agile (Scrum)

How it works. Agile is not one method but a family of methods built on the Agile Manifesto, which values individuals and interactions, working software, collaboration with the customer, and responding to change (Beck et al., 2001). Scrum is the most widely used of them. Work is listed in a product backlog and delivered in sprints of one month or less. Each sprint starts with sprint planning, has a short daily meeting, and ends with a sprint review, where the team shows what it built, and a retrospective, where it reflects on how it worked. The team has three accountabilities: a product owner, a Scrum master and the developers (Schwaber and Sutherland, 2020).

Strengths. Something working is delivered regularly, so feedback is constant and priorities can change between sprints. The retrospective builds reflection into the routine, which I find very close to what a research blog does.

Weaknesses. Scrum assumes a team. The roles, daily meetings and reviews make little sense for one person. It also focuses on delivering features, which can push research questions aside if every sprint must ship something.

For my project. I will borrow the rhythm, not the ceremony: fixed two-week cycles, a simple backlog of tasks, and a short review and reflection at the end of each cycle.

05 · Iterative

How it works. Iterative development is the simplest idea of the five: build a rough version, test it, learn from it, and use what you learned to build the next version. Each version, or iteration, is a complete loop of design, making and evaluation. It is often combined with incremental development, where each iteration also adds a new part to the product. Larman and Basili (2003) show that this way of working has been used since the 1950s, and that, despite their differences, all the iterative approaches share one aim: avoiding a single-pass, document-driven process.

The stages of one iteration. Each iteration is a self-contained mini-project with its own requirements, design, implementation and testing (Larman, 2004). In the Rational Unified Process, one iteration is even described as a small waterfall in itself (Kruchten, 2003). Different sources split the cycle into four to six stages, but the most common version has five:

  1. Plan. Decide the goal of this iteration: which question it should answer or which feature it should add, what the biggest risk is, and what “good enough” looks like. In the first round this follows a short initial planning phase, where the overall aim, constraints and deadlines of the whole project are set.
  2. Design. Turn the goal into a concrete plan for this version: sketches, user flows, a system diagram or a paper prototype. In early iterations this stays rough on purpose, because it may be thrown away.
  3. Build. Make a working version, usually only the part that is needed to answer this iteration’s question. It is a functional slice, not a polished product.
  4. Test. Check that the version works technically, and then put it in front of real people and observe how they use it. This is where most of the surprises come from.
  5. Evaluate. Review the results against the goal set in the planning stage: what worked, what did not, and why. These findings become the input for planning the next iteration.

The cycle then starts again from stage one, now with better knowledge. When the evaluation shows that the product meets its goals, or the deadline arrives, the final version leaves the loop and is finished, documented and delivered.

Strengths. It welcomes change, because each iteration is a chance to correct direction. Early versions can be very rough, a paper prototype or a quick code sketch, so mistakes are cheap. And every iteration produces evidence: what was tested, with whom, what happened and what changed.

Weaknesses. Without discipline it can loop forever, with each version slightly different but never finished. It needs clear goals for each iteration and a fixed end date.

Why I choose iterative

For my final project, I will work iteratively. The main reason is that, as in practice-based research, I will not know the answer at the start; the making is how I find it. Schön (1983) calls this “reflection-in-action”: a practitioner learns by having a conversation with the situation, trying something, seeing how it “talks back”, and adjusting. A linear plan would force me to pretend I know the outcome in week one. Iteration turns each prototype into a small experiment, and each round of user feedback into evidence I can document in this blog.

How I will make it work

To avoid the weakness of iterating forever, I will borrow structure from the other methods:

  • From the linear model: a simple overall timeline with fixed deadlines for each iteration and for the final submission.
  • From the V-model: for every iteration, write down the goal and how I will test it before I start building.
  • From the spiral: begin each iteration by naming the biggest risk, and test that first.
  • From agile: two-week cycles, a small task backlog, and a short review and reflection at the end of each cycle.

My plan is about five iterations. The first will be rough and fast, testing the core idea. The middle ones will test the main technology and the interaction with real users. The last will focus on refinement and finishing. Each iteration will follow the five stages above, and the evaluate stage will become a blog post recording what I made, what I tested, what I learned and what I will change next.
