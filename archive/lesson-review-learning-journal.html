Course Review

Practice-Based Research Methods

This lesson provided an in-depth introduction to the core concepts and research pathways of Practice-Based Research (PBR), offering a more systematic understanding of this methodology. First, we understand the differences between PBR and traditional theoretical/experimental research. Traditional research typically focuses on written texts and theories, while PBR emphasizes “learning by doing” and “exploring the unknown through practice.” This approach is particularly suitable for fields like art, design, and creative computing, where creation itself involves complex thinking and exploration.

We studied Frayling’s classic classifications

  1. Research into Practice: For example, analyzing historical architectural styles to guide design.
  2. Research through Practice: Using creative experiments (e.g., exploring new interaction methods) to uncover new knowledge.
  3. Research for Practice: Focused on developing better tools or materials to support creation.
    These classifications highlight that creation can be both a result and a research process in itself.

The course also emphasized the importance of reflective practice, requiring researchers to continuously document and reflect on their creative processes, such as why a design decision was made, challenges encountered, and solutions adopted. These records help transform “tacit knowledge” into clear, systematic insights.

Finally, there is a key aspect of PBR is verifiability: others must be able to understand your research logic and process through your documentation, ensuring the academic value of your work.

Learning Journal

Practical Research Methods

Through this week’s study, I have gained a deeper appreciation for the idea that “practice generates knowledge.” I used to think of research as writing papers and checking materials, while creation was merely “making things”, the two seemed separate. But now I realize this division is limiting. For example, when I previously created a small AR interactive installation, although I didn’t do much written summarization, the process of continuously adjusting interaction logic and testing different light effects was a form of “research” in itself. If I had systematically recorded and summarized these experiences at the time, they could have formed a valuable research case.

I am particularly drawn to the concept of “reflective practice,” which emphasizes that creators should not just “do,” but also constantly ask themselves: “Why do this?” This not only adds depth to the work but also helps me extract transferable knowledge from each project. For instance, when working on the upcoming AR project for Winchester Cathedral, I can write short reflections after each design phase, explaining why I chose a specific interaction and what I learned from user feedback.

The concept of “documentation” in the course also inspired me greatly. In the past, I only presented the final work, but the process including progress photos, notes, video records, and even failed test scenarios is a precious resource. These not only help me review the project but also allow others to better understand my thought process during presentations.

I have found that a key challenge of PBR (Practical-Based Research) is balancing creative freedom with the need for systematic organization to extract theoretical value. This is a balance I need to continuously practice.

Next, as I prepare for the Winchester Cathedral project, I plan to make “reflection + documentation” an integral part of my workflow. I will record every small achievement, not just to showcase results, but to turn the process into valuable research content.
