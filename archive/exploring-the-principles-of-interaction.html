My Interaction Manifesto

12 jan 20252025 年 1 月 12 日 · 3 min read约 3 分钟读完

这篇文章写于在学校的课程期间，原文是英文。

Overview

Interaction lies at the heart of the creative experience. In my Interaction Manifesto, I’ve summarised several guiding principles for designing meaningful interactions. In this blog post, I’ll delve deep into three key ideas: the balance between freedom and structure, the importance of feedback, and the necessity of immersive design.

(Source: Knowmax, n.d., Knowmax, Available at: https://knowmax.ai/blog/create-customer-interactions/)

In interactive experiences, participants need both the freedom to explore and structured guidance. Interaction without structure may seem aimless, while interaction lacking freedom can appear rigid and uninspired. This is well demonstrated in the project “Uncle Roy All Around You.” It is a mixed reality game that combines real-world navigation and digital cues. Participants are free to explore their surroundings, but clear direction is provided through goal-oriented tasks, such as searching for Uncle Roy. This balance not only gives participants the autonomy to act but also keeps them engaged, which is a hallmark of excellent interactive design (Graham, 2005).

Feedback is at the core of any interactive system. It transforms actions into reactions and sustains participants’ interest. For a system to seem alive and relevant, it must respond meaningfully to users’ inputs. For example, in the early media experiment project “Hole in Space,” people in two cities were connected in real-time, enabling instant visual feedback between strangers. The responses from the other side of the screen created a magical sense of connection. This feedback loop was so engaging that participants started planning meetings and reconnecting with long-lost relatives, demonstrating the immense power of responsive interaction (Galloway, 2004).

Immersive experiences should make full use of the environmental characteristics. An interaction should not only be engaging but also take full advantage of the surrounding environment, integrating the interaction and the environment to deepen the sense of participation. For example, Improv Everywhere’s “Grand Central Freeze” created an immersive environment by having participants suddenly freeze, forming a sharp contrast with the busy station. This project made full use of the environment, the real physical space, and human participation to successfully create an unforgettable and impactful interaction (Mackenzie, 2009).

Interaction design must find a balance between freedom and structure, provide meaningful feedback, and fully utilize the characteristics of the surrounding environment to create meaningful and engaging experiences.
