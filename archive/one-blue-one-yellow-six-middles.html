Week 2 Reading: One Colour, Many Mixes

10 oct 20262026 年 10 月 10 日

这篇文章写于在学校的课程期间，原文是英文。

In my first post on Carolyn L. Kane’s Chromatic Algorithms, I mentioned in one sentence that when I mixed the same blue and yellow in plain RGB maths, the middle turned a dull grey, but in a perceptual colour space (OKLCH) it stayed vivid. I kept coming back to that sentence, because it suggests something strange: one colour can have several recipes.

One pair, many middles

I kept the two ends fixed, pure screen blue #0000ff and pure yellow #ffff00, and only changed the space the browser mixes in (W3C, 2024). Plain sRGB averages the numbers and lands exactly on grey. OKLab, the perceptual space, gives a soft misty blue, because its straight line passes near the grey centre (Ottosson, 2020). OKLCH walks around the hue circle instead and finds a bright teal. Asked to go the long way round, it passes through hot pink. None of these is wrong. Each answers a different question: average the numbers, keep the lightness, or keep the colourfulness.

Why this matters to Kane’s argument

As children we learn that blue and yellow paint make green, so “mixing” feels like a natural fact. On screen it is not. Kane argues that digital colour is produced through code, hardware and standards, and that standardisation slowly hides the choices behind it (Kane, 2014). The grey gradient is a perfect small example. For years it was simply the default on the web. Nobody chose grey on purpose; it came from the maths underneath, and most people, including me, never asked.

What unsettled me most is that the vivid OKLCH middle is outside what a normal sRGB screen can show, so the browser has to squeeze it into range. The colour I “mixed” never fully appears. The screen gives me an approximation and presents it as the colour.

What I take into my practice

This also changed how I see the Bézier palette tool I built during my internship. I smoothed colours in one space without asking whether another space would give a completely different idea of “smooth”. The lesson is not to always use OKLCH. The grey middle might suit a calm or broken mood, and the pink detour could be a deliberate surprise. What matters is choosing the method instead of inheriting it. Next, I would like to let a sensor or a person’s touch decide the mixing space, so the colour between two points depends on who is interacting.
