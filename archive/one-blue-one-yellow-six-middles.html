Week 2 Experiment: One Blue, One Yellow, Six Middles

10 oct 20262026 年 10 月 10 日

这篇文章写于在学校的课程期间，原文是英文。

In my reading post on Chromatic Algorithms I wrote one quick sentence: when I mixed the same blue and yellow in plain RGB maths the middle turned grey, but in OKLCH it stayed vivid. This week I wanted to slow that sentence down. If two fixed colours can give a different middle depending on the maths, then “mixing” is not one thing. It is a choice, and usually a choice someone else made for me.

The question

Kane argues that colour on a computer is not found but produced, through code, hardware and standards (Kane, 2014). So my question was simple: if I keep the two end colours exactly the same and only change the mixing method, how many different “middles” can I get, and which one is the “real” mix?

Set-up

I chose the most extreme pair I could: pure screen blue #0000ff and pure screen yellow #ffff00. In RGB they are exact opposites, so any problem in the maths should show clearly. Modern CSS lets a gradient choose which colour space it mixes in (W3C, 2024), so I made one gradient per method and changed nothing else:

background: linear-gradient(to right in srgb,  #0000ff, #ffff00);
background: linear-gradient(to right in oklch, #0000ff, #ffff00);
/* …the same line again for srgb-linear, oklab, hsl
      and “oklch longer hue” */

Then I worked out the exact 50% colour of each one with a small Python script, using Ottosson’s published OKLab formulas (Ottosson, 2020), so I was not just trusting my eyes.

Results

These bars are not screenshots. They are drawn live by your browser, so you are running the experiment while you read. The small line marks the middle.

srgb

#808080 · flat grey, no colour at all

srgb-linear

#bcbcbc · a lighter grey

oklab

#6cabc7 · a soft, misty sky blue

oklch (shorter hue)

#00cfbd · a bright teal-green

oklch (longer hue)

#ff3185 · hot pink, going the other way round

hsl

#00ff80 · neon spring green

If all six bars look the same, your browser does not support colour-space gradients yet. The hex codes above are still the true middles.

Observations

  • sRGB takes the average of each number: (0, 0, 255) and (255, 255, 0) meet at (128, 128, 128). Mathematically correct, visually dead. The colour simply cancels itself out.
  • Linear RGB averages the actual light, not the encoded numbers, so the grey is lighter. More “physical”, but still grey.
  • OKLab was the surprise. It is the perceptual space, yet the middle is a soft, misty blue rather than vivid. Because blue and yellow sit on almost opposite sides, the straight line between them passes close to the grey centre.
  • OKLCH does not draw a straight line. It keeps the colourfulness and walks around the hue circle, so the middle becomes a bright teal-green. Choosing “longer hue” sends it the other way round, through hot pink.
  • HSL also goes round the wheel, but it gives a neon green that looks much brighter than both ends.

One more detail I did not expect: the OKLCH middle my script calculated is outside what an ordinary sRGB screen can show. The browser has to squeeze it back into range. The most vivid middle does not fully exist on my screen; the screen hands me an approximation.

Reflection

Before this test I thought of “mixing” as a fact, like two paints in a jar. Most of us learn that blue and yellow paint make green. On screen, the same idea gives grey, misty blue, teal, pink or neon green, and none of them is wrong. Each one answers a different question: average the numbers, average the light, keep the perceived lightness, keep the colourfulness. So a colour does not have one recipe. It has several, and the method is a design decision.

This links back to Kane in a very practical way. Her point about standardisation is that the defaults hide the choices (Kane, 2014). For years, the default gradient on the web was the grey one. Nobody chose grey on purpose; it came from the maths underneath. It also made me look again at my own Bézier palette tool from my internship. I was smoothing colours in one space without asking whether another space would give a completely different “smooth”.

For my practice, the useful lesson is not “always use OKLCH”. The grey middle could be exactly right for a calm or broken feeling, and the pink detour could be a deliberate surprise. Next, I want to let a sensor or a person’s touch choose the mixing method itself, so the space between two colours changes depending on who is interacting.
