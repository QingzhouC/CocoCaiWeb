Week 1 Reading: The Reflective Practitioner

30 sep 20262026 年 9 月 30 日

这篇文章写于在学校的课程期间，原文是英文。

This week was the start of Experimental Practice & Research, and the first reading I chose was Donald Schön’s The Reflective Practitioner (1983). It is an old book, but it asks a question that felt very current to me at the start of a year of research: how do people who make things actually know what they know?

Beyond applying theory

Schön argues against what he calls technical rationality, the idea that professionals simply take scientific theory and apply it to problems (Schön, 1983). In real practice, problems do not arrive neatly defined. He describes a “high, hard ground” where problems can be solved with research-based techniques, and a “swampy lowland” where situations are messy and confusing, and where, he suggests, the most important problems usually are.

What I found most useful is his idea of knowing-in-action. A lot of what skilled people know is tacit: they can do it, but cannot fully explain it. When something unexpected happens, good practitioners do not stop and go back to theory. They reflect while they are still acting, test a new move, and see what happens. Schön calls this reflection-in-action, and describes design as a “reflective conversation with the situation”, where the work itself “talks back”.

What this means for me

This changed how I see a research blog. If much of my knowledge is tacit, then writing after each experiment is not just reporting. It is how I turn knowing-in-action into something I can look at and share. Schön also made me notice something he takes for granted: the conversation only works if someone is listening. The situation can “talk back”, but the signal is often small, and it is easy to miss when a change happens slowly or quietly.

That thought shaped the rest of my week. I wanted to look at a real situation where things change but people struggle to notice, and the first one that came to mind was living with an ageing pet. I explore this in my research post later this week. Schön’s idea that knowledge comes from cycles of making and reflecting also raised a practical question: what kind of development process would support that? That became my other research topic this week.
